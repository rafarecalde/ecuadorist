This is not medical advice. It is how cover is organized, so a new resident knows which door is which.

<div class="compare cols-3">
<article>
<h3>IESS</h3>
<p>The public social-security system. Voluntary affiliation is for people with income and no employer, living in Ecuador. A foreigner needs an Ecuadorian identity card, or a refugee card, and must not owe the institute money. You also have to be 18 or older. The institute’s own list of benefits includes illness and maternity care, a pension, funeral aid, work-risk cover, health care for children under 18, and a mortgage loan. Cover for a spouse can be added by paying an extra premium the institute states as 3.41 percent.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>Private hospitals</h3>
<p>In Quito and Cuenca, foreign residents generally use private clinics. Hospital Metropolitano in Quito is a long-established private hospital. Cuenca has several of its own. Outside those cities, a specialist often means a drive. Some doctors in the two cities speak English. A public pharmacy is a different bet. Fybeca is one of the national chains. Carry the generic names of medicines you cannot interrupt.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>International plans</h3>
<p>A policy bought abroad. On the pensioner, rentista, and remote-worker visa pages, Ecuador asks for health insurance that is valid for the whole visa, and a foreign policy has to say it covers you in Ecuador. SafetyWing’s Nomad Insurance is the travel medical plan. Nomad Insurance Complete is the long-stay plan. Genki sells monthly worldwide cover. Cigna Global and IMG are international insurers. The contract, not this page, is the policy.</p>
</article>
</div>

Quito sits near 2,850 meters. That is a medical fact for anyone with a heart or lung condition: talk to your own doctor before a move. For the [Amazon](/visit/the-amazon/), the CDC’s traveler page is the reference for yellow fever and mosquito-borne illness.
