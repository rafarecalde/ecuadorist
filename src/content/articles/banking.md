Ecuador has used the US dollar since 2000. Visitors do not change money. Coins include familiar US pieces and Ecuadorian centavo coins in the same sizes. Bills are US bills. A fifty can be awkward at a market stall.

<div class="compare cols-3">
<article>
<h3>A US card</h3>
<p>The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says some prepaid cards charge a foreign transaction fee outside the United States, and that the cardholder agreement is where the fee is written. Card agreements filed with the bureau show the same split: one sample lists a foreign transaction fee, another lists none. A “no foreign fee” card is the one whose agreement says none. Read that page before you travel. Revolut’s own schedule is the place to see what its card charges.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>An ATM</h3>
<p>ATMs are common in cities and scarce on back roads. A bureau disclosure notes that the ATM operator can add its own fee, on top of whatever your card issuer charges. The screen is the moment to read both. Withdrawal limits are often modest, so people make more than one stop. Tell your bank you are traveling. The Saturday market in Otavalo is still a cash place.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>A local account</h3>
<p>Major banks include Banco Pichincha, Banco Guayaquil, Produbanco, and Banco del Pacífico. A local account usually waits on residency and a cédula, the national identity card, plus whatever proof of address that bank asks for this year. A tourist should not build a plan that depends on opening one in the first week. Keep the US account.</p>
</article>
</div>

Counterfeits exist wherever dollars circulate. Glance at larger bills. Do not carry a month of cash because an ATM felt far away. Moving the month’s expenses from the United States is the [money chapter](/retire/moving-money/). Rent deposits are in the [renting chapter](/retire/renting/): never wire one for a home you have not seen.
