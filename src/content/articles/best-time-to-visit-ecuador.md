Ecuador sits on the equator, so “summer” is the wrong word. Elevation and ocean currents do the work that latitude does not.

<div class="compare cols-2">
<article>
<h3>Sierra</h3>
<p>Britannica describes a long dry season from June to September, rains that peak around the equinoxes, and a shorter dry spell, the veranillo, from December through January. Quito’s afternoons still cloud over. <a href="/visit/cotopaxi/">Cotopaxi</a> and <a href="/visit/quilotoa/">Quilotoa</a> are clearer when they are clearer, not on a guarantee.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>Coast</h3>
<p>The same source gives the coast a wet season in the first half of the year and a drier second half. The UK FCDO, writing about the country as a whole, says the rainy season usually runs from October to May and can last into June. Heavy rain closes roads. The <a href="/visit/is-ecuador-safe/">safety chapter</a> is the other reason to treat a beach month as a decision, not a default.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>Amazon</h3>
<p>Britannica calls the Oriente’s rain fairly continuous. A lodge week does not wait for a dry month that the climate description does not offer. The choice between <a href="/visit/amazon-cuyabeno-yasuni-tena/">Cuyabeno, Yasuní, and Tena</a> is access and advising, not a secret sunny week.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>Galápagos</h3>
<p>Galápagos Conservancy describes two seasons: warm and wetter from December to May, then the cooler garúa season from June to November, with mist on the highlands. Wildlife does not leave. The <a href="/visit/galapagos/">islands chapter</a> is the rest of the plan.</p>
</article>
</div>

## Month by month

The blocks below follow those sources. They are not a rainfall table. INAMHI publishes the forecast.

<div class="compare cols-2">
<article>
<h3>June to September</h3>
<p>Sierra: the long dry season. Coast: the drier half of the year. Amazon: still wet. Galápagos: garúa, cooler water, mist above the shore. A highland <a href="/visit/ten-days-in-ecuador/">ten days</a> fits this window.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>October and November</h3>
<p>FCDO’s country rainy season is underway. Sierra rains build toward the pattern Britannica ties to the equinoxes. Coast is leaving its drier half. Galápagos is still in garúa until December.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>December and January</h3>
<p>Sierra: the shorter veranillo. Coast: into the wetter first half. Galápagos: the warm season starts. This is also when many people try to add the islands to a <a href="/visit/two-weeks-ecuador-galapagos/">two-week trip</a>.</p>
</article>
<article>
<h3>February to May</h3>
<p>Sierra: the wetter stretch around the March equinox. Coast: the wet half. Galápagos: warm season through May, then garúa. Roads in the mountains are the thing that fails, not the wildlife.</p>
</article>
</div>

## El Niño, 2026–2027

The FCDO’s October 2, 2026 update says Ecuadorian authorities have issued a red alert ahead of a forecast El Niño. The expectation on that page is unusually heavy rain, possible flooding and landslides, and higher temperatures. Britannica’s climate entry describes the same kind of event: warm water off the coast, and downpours that can reach the highlands. A red alert is not a cancelled country. It is a reason to read the government’s El Niño page, in Spanish, and to keep a highland day loose.

Landslides are ordinary even in a normal rainy season. The FCDO says heavy rain frequently closes roads.
